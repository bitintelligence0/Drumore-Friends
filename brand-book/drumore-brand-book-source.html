Before we start

What the archive says about the brief

The brief was written before anyone looked at the material. The archive changes several of its assumptions.

These findings come from the Facebook archive captured on 14–15 October 2026 and the curation notes made from it. They shape everything that follows.

1

The name is Drumore, and “Friends” means Quakers.

The brief says “Drewmore Friends.”

The sign at the meeting house reads “Drumore Friends Meeting House, Established 1816.” Friends here are members of the Religious Society of Friends, so this is a meeting’s history, not a “friends of” society. Beth’s posts use both “Meeting House” (56 times) and “Meetinghouse” (66). This book follows the sign until she chooses.

2

The record runs 1683–1916, with today attached.

The brief says roughly 1600 to the present.

A straight 1600–2026 axis would put most of the screen over empty years and squeeze the dense diary years into a sliver. The timeline uses eras of planned width, with visible folds where time is compressed.

3

Quaker dates need their own rules.

The brief treats dates as plain numbers.

Minutes are dated like “9th of 11th month, 1811.” Before 1752 the year began in March, so “9th month” meant November. Beth explains this in one of her own posts. Every date keeps what was written beside the modern reading.

4

The story starts somewhere else.

The brief asks for a tightly focused local map.

Twelve of the 37 events happen in Germantown, Chester County or Gwynedd before the families reach Drumore. The system has two map scales: a detailed Drumore map and a schematic journey map.

5

Visitors arrive from Facebook, on phones.

The brief’s references are desktop showpieces.

Beth’s readers tap a link in their feed. The phone is the primary layout, with time running down the screen, and every story needs a good link preview.

6

The archive is rich in people and pictures.

The brief wants Histography’s density.

Thirty-seven dots would look empty. The density comes from people. Each life is drawn as a line with a portrait at its start, and the family tree shows how those lives connect.

7

Nothing is verified yet, and some sources disagree.

The brief asks us not to imply certainty.

Some of Beth’s posts disagree. One says Wynona’s mother died “when she was 3,” and another says 1874. A reunion photo is captioned 1910 while the event list says 1912. The design shows both readings until the record settles them.

Why the timeline can’t be a straight ruler

Real data: the 37 dated event candidates in curation/timeline-events.json. Era names are a first draft for Beth to rename.

Three directions

Three different answers to the brief, then one recommendation.

Recommended

A · Plain Light

The Quaker idea of plainness, used as a working design method rather than a costume. The site feels like the meeting house on an open-house morning: limewashed walls, daylight, walnut benches and dark green shutters. The interface stays quiet and exact, so the photographs and documents carry all the age and texture. Each person’s life is a fine line through time that lights up in lamp amber when you follow it.

Palette
Type

Gelasio, a sturdy Georgia-style serif, for everything that is read. Public Sans for every control and date.

Defining device

Lifelines: one line per person, a portrait at its start, knots for events and a fade where the record runs out.

Main risk

Restraint can turn into blandness. It depends on good photographs, confident scale and the dark green and brown bands to give the page rhythm.

B · The Minute Book

The meeting’s minute book becomes the interface. Everything is a numbered entry in a ruled ledger: the date in Quaker form, the minute, the clerk’s hand. Stories grow as new minutes are added under old ones. It is honest and scholarly, and the clearest of the three about sources.

Palette
Type

Newsreader for entries, IBM Plex Mono for dates and folio numbers.

Defining device

The ruled entry: a date column, a margin rule and a numbered minute.

Main risk

It is the research dashboard the brief rules out, and ruled paper drifts toward the faux-parchment look. It rewards scholars and loses the Facebook visitor.

C · Lantern Show

A darkened room and a magic-lantern evening. Photographs glow out of a deep field as you drift through time, closest in spirit to Histography. Selecting a photograph brings it forward, and its story is read beneath it like a lecture caption. It would have the most impact on first view.

Palette
Type

Bodoni Moda italic for names, Work Sans for captions.

Defining device

The glowing plate: photographs lit from behind in a dark field.

Main risk

Darkness reads as a memorial and is hard on older eyes. Warm glow on black drifts into sepia nostalgia, and it is the hardest to make comfortable on a phone.

Why Plain Light

It is the only direction that comes from this specific place. Quaker plainness is a real testimony of simplicity, and the meeting house is a white building with green shutters, not a museum. Restraint here is the subject’s own aesthetic. Plain Light also handles the brief’s two hardest demands: someone arriving from Facebook understands it at once, and it can show uncertainty without looking broken.

Chapter 2

Brand idea

The idea, the personality and the positioning.

Held in the Light

Friends speak of “holding someone in the Light,” meaning to keep them in mind with care. The site does the same for every person in the archive. Each life is drawn plainly, kept in view and treated with care. Where the record is dark, we say so.

This phrase has religious meaning for Friends. Ask Beth, and the meeting if she thinks it right, whether it is welcome as a public line. If not, keep it as an internal idea and use the positioning statement publicly.

Positioning

Drumore Friends is the lasting home for the stories of the Drumore Friends Meeting House and the families connected to it: a timeline you can walk through, from the Germantown ancestors to the people who still open its doors. Every photograph and date says where it came from.

PlainSays it once, in ordinary words, at a comfortable size.Not austere, not minimal for show.

Chapter 3

Visual principles

What must always be true across the site. Each has a test to apply to a screen.

Daylight, not age

The interface is limewash, plaster and daylight, with dark green and brown bands for rhythm. It never imitates paper, sepia or wear. Only the photographs and documents carry age.

Test: hide every image. If the screen still looks like 1890, it is wearing a costume.

The record is the ornament

No flourishes, borders or textures. A cabinet card’s mount, a minute book’s ruling and a diary’s handwriting are the decoration, shown whole and at a generous size.

Test: any decorative element that did not come from the archive should go.

Show the edges of what we know

Every date shows its precision and every claim shows its evidence. Lines fade where the record runs out. Uncertainty is drawn with the same care as fact.

Test: can a visitor tell “recorded,” “reported” and “family story” apart without reading a help page?

One room, many doors

Timeline, family tree, person, story and place are views of the same things. Moving between them never loses your place, and there is always a single way back.

Test: from any screen, can you get back to the year you started from in one tap?

Plain speech

Controls say what they do: “Follow her life,” “See the original,” “Where was this?”

Test: would Beth’s oldest reader know what every button does?

Quotations

“Let thy religion be simple, plain, and full of love for thy fellow mortals.”Will Lamborn to his daughter, 1873, as quoted in archive post P035.

Keep the original spelling and punctuation. Don’t add [sic]. A quotation always links to its scan.

Long stories

  • One column, 60–66 characters a line, 19px. Never multiple columns.
  • Break the story into scenes at every new date, each with a Walnut date line.
  • A picture, quotation or source card every three to five paragraphs.
  • Plain Gelasio headings. No drop caps or ornaments.
  • Footnotes become source cards in the margin on desktop and tappable markers on phones.
Chapter 6

The mark

The Gable Line. Approved in direction; refine only line weights and the smallest sizes.

One line runs along the ground, rises into the meeting house roof and runs on, like a building standing on a timeline. The two small windows are the gable-end shutters. It is traced from the building itself, so it is the meeting house, simplified until it could be drawn with one stroke.

Why it works

It’s literal without being a picture. Anyone who has stood at the gable end recognizes it, and anyone who hasn’t still reads “a plain building on a line through time.”

Why the windows matter

Two windows give the house a face. That small bit of personality makes it friendly rather than corporate, and it’s what makes it this meeting house rather than any house.

Why one line

The ground line continues past the house on both sides. It is the same line as the timeline, so the logo already says what the site does.

Chapter 7

Photographs and documents

Show originals whole and true. Mark anything new as new.

The provenance tab

Every image sits on a plain mat with a small tab on its lower edge. The tab answers one question before any caption: what am I looking at?

Original

A photograph, document or object from the time, shown as found. Solid frame. Square Walnut marker.

Drawn

Anything made now: illustrations, transcriptions, reconstructions, maps. Dashed River frame. Always says who made it and from what.

Today

Recent photographs: open houses, the cemetery, preservation work. Round Shutter marker. Always dated.

Chapter 8

A · Everyday hand (Kalam). A relaxed, slanted print hand. The easiest to read, and the friendliest. Recommended.

Avoid · joined script. Looks the most “old,” but loops and joins slow every reader down. The originals already show the real hand.

How transcriptions get made

  • Claude reads the scan and drafts the transcription, marking any word it isn’t sure of.
  • Beth checks the draft against the page and resolves the marked words.
  • Names get editorial tags linking to the person’s page and family tree.
  • Each transcription records who checked it and when.

Tested on this archive

Claude transcribed the diary spread above and Will’s 1873 letter. On the diary, its reading matched the transcription Beth posted in P045 word for word, except for one word she had dropped (“moved in on Oct. 11”). Clear 1890s hands transcribe well. Faded pencil, 1700s minutes and damaged pages will need more of Beth’s eye.

Chapter 10

Maps

The timeline laid flat: same markers, same amber, same rules about certainty.

Style study · not a real place

Styling

  • Fields in two near tones (#E4E7DC, #DCE1D2) with hedgerow edges. The farmland is the texture.
  • Woods #CDD7C4, water #BCD1D5. Flat, with no terrain shading or satellite imagery.
  • Water names in Gelasio italic, everything else in Public Sans.

Time on the map

The map always says which year it shows and what the base map is. Historic maps are opt-in overlays labeled Original with their date. Moving the year only changes features that have a source for that year.

Map and timeline share

The same evidence markers, the same amber for the selected person’s places, and one “Showing 1890” chip that drives both. Opening the map from a scene shows the return pill.

Making the real maps

  1. Geography from records. In Figma, trace roads, streams and township lines from Lancaster County GIS. Place historic features from the 1864, 1875 and 1899 maps.
  2. Style from prompts. Use image generation only for texture and illustration style (field patterns, tree clusters, building pictograms), never for where things are.
  3. Apply the tokens. Swap every color to the map tokens and export one SVG layer per era.
  4. Label it Drawn. Every map carries a Drawn tab: “Drawn 2026, from the 1864 Bridgens map and county GIS.”

Chapter 13

Motion, desktop and phone

Movement explains where you are. The phone layout comes first.

What moves, and why

  • Selection. The chosen lifeline or tree branch brightens to Lamp and the rest fade to Mist.
  • Travel. Jumping to a year pans the field there, capped at 420ms.
  • Opening. A card grows into its story from where it sat and shrinks back on return.
  • Nothing else. No parallax, scroll-triggered reveals, autoplay or Ken Burns pans on photographs.

Try it

Tap a line, or use Tab and Enter. With reduced motion on, the change is instant.

On her twenty-second birthday, Wynona begins a diary

She had finished teacher training at Millersville and was teaching at the one-room Conowingo School in Fulton Township. That was too far to travel every day, so during the week she lived with Aunt Alice and Uncle Will Shoemaker. On weekends she went home to Aunt Lib and Uncle Thomas at Fernglen.

Her first entry is the longest she ever wrote. “How time flies!” she begins. She kept it up every day until 12 May 1891.

Chapter 16

Accessibility and performance

A public history site is for everyone in the family, including the oldest members on the slowest phones.

Accessibility · WCAG 2.2 AA minimum

  • Text meets AA everywhere and AAA for body text. Lamp is never text on light grounds.
  • Evidence, precision and provenance are always a shape plus a word.
  • The timeline and family tree each have a “Browse as a list” equivalent with real headings.
  • Keyboard: Tab through people in order, arrows move along a life or branch, Enter opens, Esc goes back. Focus is a 3px Lamp ring.
  • Every handwritten page has a plain-text transcription. It is the accessible version and the searchable one.
  • Text enlarges to 200% without breaking. Stories are at least 18px.
  • Living people appear only with consent, and each person page links to “Suggest a correction.”

Performance · a mid-range phone on 4G

  • Every person, story and place has a real server-rendered URL with link-preview tags.
  • Largest content shown within 2.5s. First screen under 300 KB before photographs.
  • Responsive AVIF or WebP images. Portrait bubbles about 8 KB, timeline thumbnails about 30 KB, full scans as zoom tiles in the viewer only.
  • Fonts: Gelasio and Public Sans subset to Latin, about 120 KB. The handwriting face loads only on transcription pages.
  • Timeline and tree in SVG up to about 1,500 marks, Canvas beyond that. No WebGL needed.
  • TIFF masters live in an archive store, separate from web copies. The website is not the archive.
Chapter 17

Do and avoid

For future designers and AI-assisted implementation. Paste this page into prompts.

Do

  • Spell it Drumore. Use “Meeting House” as on the sign until Beth decides.
  • Use Gelasio for everything read, Public Sans for everything operated.
  • Use Limewash grounds with Shutter and Bench bands for rhythm.
  • Show every original whole, in its own color, with an Original tab.
  • Put a transcription beside every handwritten page.
  • Label every new image, map and transcription Drawn, with its maker and sources.
  • Give every date its precision and its “as written” form.
  • Draw people as lifelines with portraits, and fade them where the record ends.
  • Show “raised by” as its own link in the family tree.
  • Show conflicts (“1872 or 1874”) and link each source.
  • Keep one amber thing per screen.
  • Design for the phone first. Leave a way back on every screen.

Avoid

  • Sepia, parchment, paper textures, torn edges, wax seals, distressed fonts.
  • High-contrast display serifs with hairlines (Caslon Display, Playfair, Didone faces).
  • Handwriting fonts in the interface, or on anything not labeled Drawn.
  • Cream grounds with a terracotta accent. That is the generic AI look.
  • Colorizing, AI-upscaling faces, generating portraits or historical scenes.
  • AI-generated geography. Prompts are for map style only.
  • Ken Burns pans, parallax, scroll-jacking, autoplay.
  • Exact pins for places known only approximately.
  • Padding partial dates or silently converting Quaker dates.
  • Color coding families. Use labeled lanes and the tree instead.
  • Crosses, steeples, doves or quills in the mark.
  • Treating Facebook posts as the unit of history. Posts are sources for stories.
